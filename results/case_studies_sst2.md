# Case Studies: Where BERT and DistilBERT Disagree on SST-2

This document is the qualitative half of the error analysis. Aggregate
numbers live in [`error_breakdown_sst2.json`](error_breakdown_sst2.json) and the
figures under [`figures_sst2/`](figures_sst2/). Here we look at *which kinds of
sentences* drive the 2.87-percentage-point accuracy gap, by reading
individual disagreements between the teacher (BERT-base) and the student
(DistilBERT-base).

The full ranked list of disagreements is generated by
[`code/extract_cases.py`](../code/extract_cases.py) and dumped to
[`case_studies_candidates_sst2.csv`](case_studies_candidates_sst2.csv); below we
hand-pick the most representative examples and label the linguistic
phenomenon at play.

---

## Summary of phenomena

Out of 872 SST-2 validation sentences, the two models disagreed on **41**
(33 BERT-only-correct, 8 DistilBERT-only-correct). Reading those 41
sentences, four recurring linguistic patterns explain most of the gap:

| Phenomenon | Cases below | Why DistilBERT loses |
|---|---|---|
| Polarity reversal via negation | §1 (3 cases) | `not / don't / haven't` flips overall sentiment but DistilBERT latches onto local positive/negative cues |
| Contrastive / concessive structure | §2 (3 cases) | the verdict sits in the second clause after `but / though`; DistilBERT weights the first clause too much |
| Sarcasm and metaphor | §3 (3 cases) | requires world knowledge; surface words point the wrong way |
| Understated / idiomatic positive | §4 (3 cases) | phrases like *almost unsurpassed*, *not the least*, *shades of gray* read as negative on the surface |

Then §5 covers the rarer reverse direction (DistilBERT correct, BERT wrong).

A striking observation across §1–§4: **DistilBERT does not just disagree —
it is over-confidently wrong.** Mean DistilBERT confidence on the 33
`bert_only_correct` cases is **0.893**, despite 0% accuracy on those
samples. This calibration failure is also visible in
[`fig7_confidence_calibration.png`](figures_sst2/fig7_confidence_calibration.png).

---

## §1. Polarity reversal via negation

These all turn on a single negation word (`don't`, `haven't`, `un-`).
DistilBERT seems to weight surface positive/negative tokens (*hilarity,
appreciate*) more than the negation that flips them.

**Case 1.1** — id 8 *(true: positive, DistilBERT: **negative**, conf 0.955)*

> "you do n't have to know about music to appreciate the film 's easygoing blend of comedy and romance ."

`don't have to` is itself a positive construction ("anyone can enjoy
this"), but DistilBERT seems to read `don't` + `know` as a negative
context.

**Case 1.2** — id 770 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.877)*

> "i do n't think i laughed out loud once ."

The surface words *laughed out loud* are strongly positive; only the
`don't think ... once` scope flips them. BERT handles the long-distance
negation; DistilBERT does not.

**Case 1.3** — id 375 *(true: positive, DistilBERT: **negative**, conf 0.502)*

> "we have n't seen such hilarity since say it is n't so !"

Two negations (*haven't seen*, *isn't so*) plus a comparative idiom — the
sentence is a strong compliment, but DistilBERT only barely commits to a
negative label (conf 0.50). Length doesn't help either: 13 words.

---

## §2. Contrastive / concessive structure

The pattern is a positive-sounding clause followed by `but` / `though` /
`while` that flips the verdict. BERT gets these consistently; DistilBERT
appears to over-weight the first clause.

**Case 2.1** — id 405 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.889)*

> "outer-space buffs might love this film , but others will find its pleasures intermittent ."

The first clause sounds enthusiastic ("might love this film"); the
verdict is in the second clause after `but`. DistilBERT stops at the
first clause.

**Case 2.2** — id 184 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.799)*

> "though perry and hurley make inspiring efforts to breathe life into the disjointed , haphazard script by jay scherick and david ronn , neither the actors nor director reginald hudlin can make it more than fitfully entertaining ."

A 38-word sentence with three negations (*disjointed*, *haphazard*,
*neither/nor*) and a `though` opener whose true conclusion is buried at
the end. This is exactly the long-and-complex case where the teacher's
extra capacity matters.

**Case 2.3** — id 213 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.963)*

> "this time mr. burns is trying something in the martin scorsese street-realist mode , but his self-regarding sentimentality trips him up again ."

Praise of ambition followed by a `but` that delivers a sharp critique.
DistilBERT is 96% sure it's positive — over-confidently wrong.

---

## §3. Sarcasm and metaphor

These cases require world knowledge or tonal interpretation: a literal
read of the surface words gives the wrong polarity.

**Case 3.1** — id 21 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.832)*

> "the iditarod lasts for days - this just felt like it did ."

The Iditarod is a multi-day Alaskan sled-dog race; saying the movie
"felt like it lasted that long" is a tedium complaint. There is no
explicit negative word — DistilBERT has nothing to anchor a negative
prediction on.

**Case 3.2** — id 704 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.953)*

> "a bloated gasbag thesis grotesquely impressed by its own gargantuan aura of self-importance ..."

A pile of negative evaluative adjectives (*bloated*, *gasbag*,
*grotesquely*, *gargantuan aura of self-importance*), but the verb
*impressed* and the absence of explicit negation seem to mislead
DistilBERT. BERT correctly picks up the cumulative tone.

**Case 3.3** — id 813 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.571)*

> "i can take infantile humor ... but this is the sort of infantile that makes you wonder about changing the director and writer 's diapers ."

Extended sarcasm: starts with a concession, escalates with a metaphor
about the filmmakers being so juvenile they need diapers. Again no
canonical negative word; the criticism is encoded in figurative
language.

---

## §4. Understated / idiomatic positive

The mirror image of §3 — sentences that look negative on the surface but
are praise. The `un-` prefix and "not + X" idioms are the main
culprits.

**Case 4.1** — id 633 *(true: positive, DistilBERT: **negative**, conf 0.946)*

> "still , as a visual treat , the film is almost unsurpassed ."

*almost unsurpassed* = essentially the highest quality. DistilBERT
appears to read the `un-` morpheme as a negation cue.

**Case 4.2** — id 692 *(true: positive, DistilBERT: **negative**, conf 0.985)*

> "sustains its dreamlike glide through a succession of cheesy coincidences and voluptuous cheap effects , not the least of which is rebecca romijn-stamos ."

The sentence opens with what sounds like a string of complaints
(*cheesy*, *cheap*) but the framing — *sustains*, *dreamlike glide*,
*not the least of which* — is positive. The idiom *not the least* means
*notably*, the opposite of its literal compositional reading.

**Case 4.3** — id 684 *(true: positive, DistilBERT: **negative**, conf 0.705)*

> "a study in shades of gray , offering itself up in subtle plot maneuvers ..."

*Shades of gray* and *subtle* sound muted/dim, but in film criticism
they are compliments (nuanced, sophisticated). DistilBERT lacks the
domain knowledge that "subtle" tends to be praise in a review context.

---

## §5. The reverse direction — when DistilBERT outperforms BERT

In 8 cases the student is right and the teacher is wrong. These suggest
DistilBERT is not strictly worse — it sometimes generalises differently.

**Case 5.1** — id 728 *(true: positive, BERT: **negative**, conf 0.803)*

> "but taken as a stylish and energetic one-shot , the queen of the damned can not be said to suck ."

A double-negative idiom (*cannot be said to suck* = is OK). BERT was
fooled by the literal *cannot* + *suck* reading; DistilBERT got it.

**Case 5.2** — id 527 *(true: negative, BERT: **positive**, conf 0.890)*

> "it has all the excitement of eating oatmeal ."

A pure metaphor: as exciting as oatmeal = boring. BERT picked up
*excitement* as positive. DistilBERT inferred the comparison.

**Case 5.3** — id 205 *(true: positive, BERT: **negative**, conf 0.697)*

> "falls neatly into the category of good stupid fun ."

The colloquial idiom *good stupid fun* is positive (light entertainment),
but BERT seems to weight *stupid* negatively. DistilBERT's wider tolerance
on this kind of phrase pays off.

These examples are too few to be statistically meaningful (n = 8 ≈ 1% of
the validation set), but they suggest the cost of distillation is
slightly noisier, not strictly monotone — the student does not simply
inherit a worse copy of the teacher's decision boundary.

## Discussion

Across the 41 disagreements, a consistent picture emerges:

1. **DistilBERT is most likely to fail when sentiment is encoded
   compositionally** — through negation, contrastive structure, sarcasm,
   or idiom — rather than through bag-of-positive/negative tokens.

2. **The student is overconfident on the cases it gets wrong.** Mean
   DistilBERT confidence on its 33 distillation-cost mistakes is 0.893.
   When DistilBERT is wrong it does not signal uncertainty, so simple
   rejection-by-confidence will not catch these errors.

3. **Length amplifies but does not cause the gap.** The accuracy gap
   grows from 1.7 pp on 5–10-word sentences to 3.1 pp on 20+-word
   sentences (see [`fig3_length_buckets.png`](figures_sst2/fig3_length_buckets.png)),
   but the qualitative pattern — failing on negation/sarcasm — appears
   at all lengths (cf. *id 449: "oh come on ."*, only 4 words).

4. **The negation gap (4.3 pp) is twice the no-negation gap (2.0 pp)**
   (see [`fig4_negation_split.png`](figures_sst2/fig4_negation_split.png)).
   This is the single most actionable finding: most of the cost of
   distillation lives in the part of the data where surface-level cues
   *contradict* the true label. If the goal is to close the gap, those
   are the examples to focus extra training signal on.
