# Case Studies: Where BERT and DistilBERT Disagree on MRPC

This document is the qualitative half of the error analysis for MRPC
(paraphrase identification). Aggregate numbers live in
[`error_breakdown_mrpc.json`](error_breakdown_mrpc.json) and the figures
under [`figures_mrpc/`](figures_mrpc/). Here we look at the individual
sentence pairs where the teacher (BERT-base) and the student
(DistilBERT-base) disagreed.

The full ranked list of disagreements is generated by
[`code/extract_cases.py`](../code/extract_cases.py) and dumped to
[`case_studies_candidates_mrpc.csv`](case_studies_candidates_mrpc.csv);
below we hand-pick representative examples and label the linguistic
phenomenon at play.

---

## Headline: the student wins overall

The first thing to flag is that on MRPC, **DistilBERT outperforms BERT**:

| | BERT | DistilBERT |
|---|---:|---:|
| Accuracy | 0.821 | **0.836** |
| F1 | 0.876 | **0.883** |
| Agreement rate | | **86.3%** |

This is the opposite direction from SST-2 (BERT +2.87 pp) and IMDb
(BERT +0.84 pp). MRPC is a much smaller training set (3,668 train pairs
vs. 25K for IMDb and 67K for SST-2), and the cleaner story is that
**the larger model overfits where the smaller one cannot**.

Out of 408 validation pairs, the two models disagreed on **56** (25
BERT-only-correct, 31 DistilBERT-only-correct). The disagreement
breakdown also tells a sharper story than aggregate accuracy:

- 25 `bert_only_correct` cases: 48% are true paraphrases (label = 1) that
  DistilBERT missed; 52% are non-paraphrases that DistilBERT
  over-confidently called paraphrase.
- 31 `distilbert_only_correct` cases: only **29%** are true paraphrases
  that BERT missed; **71%** are non-paraphrases that BERT incorrectly
  called paraphrase.

So the dominant failure mode here is **BERT over-predicting paraphrase
when two sentences share a lot of surface material but encode different
facts.** DistilBERT is more conservative and gets credit for it.

---

## §1. BERT-only correct — what trips the student up

These 25 cases split between (a) genuine paraphrases that DistilBERT
incorrectly called *not* a paraphrase, and (b) non-paraphrases that
DistilBERT over-confidently called paraphrase. The recurring patterns:

### §1.1 Numeric / quantitative mismatch ignored

**Case 1.1** — id 157 *(true: not paraphrase, DistilBERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.816)*

> s1: "The blasts killed two people and injured more than 150 others."
> s2: "The Atlanta Olympic Games attack killed one woman and injured
>     more than 100 other people."

Different events with different casualty counts (2/150 vs. 1/100).
DistilBERT latches onto the structural overlap (*killed X and injured
more than Y*) and ignores the numbers.

**Case 1.2** — id 171 *(true: paraphrase, DistilBERT: **not paraphrase**, conf 0.757)*

> s1: "At least 27 US troops have been killed in hostile fire since Bush's statement."
> s2: "At least 26 American troops have been killed in hostile fire since major combat was officially declared over on May 1."

The mirror-image failure: 27 vs 26 *is* small enough that MRPC labels
this a paraphrase, but DistilBERT treats the numeric difference as
disqualifying. The student's discrimination rule is "different number =
not paraphrase," which under-fits to MRPC's annotator judgement.

### §1.2 Speaker / source attribution

**Case 1.3** — id 338 *(true: not paraphrase, DistilBERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.804)*

> s1: "We strongly disagree with Novell's position and view it as a
>     desperate measure to curry favor with the Linux community."
> s2: "McBride characterized Novell's move as 'a desperate measure to
>     curry favor with the Linux community.'"

The exact phrase *desperate measure to curry favor with the Linux
community* appears in both, but s1 is a first-person company statement
("we strongly disagree") and s2 is a third-person attribution to a named
person ("McBride characterized"). DistilBERT keys on the shared quoted
material; BERT picks up the speaker shift.

### §1.3 Asymmetric content / one sentence is a superset

**Case 1.4** — id 180 *(true: not paraphrase, DistilBERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.771)*

> s1: "The chain operates more than 3,400 stores, and has annual revenue
>     of about $15.8 billion."
> s2: "The chain, which has been under new management since late 1999,
>     has more than 3,400 stores and $15.8 billion in annual revenue."

s2 carries an additional dated factual claim ("under new management
since late 1999") that s1 does not — enough for the gold label to be
*not paraphrase*. BERT catches the extra clause; DistilBERT calls it
paraphrase on the strength of the matching numbers.

## §2. DistilBERT-only correct — where BERT over-predicts paraphrase

The larger of the two disagreement categories (n = 31). 71% of these are
non-paraphrases that BERT incorrectly called paraphrases. This is the
core of the cross-model story.

### §2.1 One sentence is a strict subset of the other

**Case 2.1** — id 382 *(true: not paraphrase, BERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.881)*

> s1: "GE stock closed Friday at $30.65 a share, down about 42 cents,
>     on the New York Stock Exchange."
> s2: "GE's shares closed at $30.65 on Friday on the New York Stock
>     Exchange."

s1 carries the change ("down about 42 cents") that s2 omits.
Annotator-wise this counts as not a paraphrase. BERT confidently disagrees
(0.88).

**Case 2.2** — id 264 *(true: not paraphrase, BERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.862)*

> s1: "Corixa shares rose 54 cents to $7.74 yesterday on the Nasdaq
>     Stock Market."
> s2: "Shares of Corixa rose 54 cents, or about 8 percent, to close at
>     $7.74."

Same shape: a price-change number is enriched in s2 with a percentage
("or about 8 percent") that does not appear in s1. BERT calls them
equivalent; DistilBERT picks up the asymmetry.

### §2.2 Tense / role / lifecycle shift

**Case 2.3** — id 158 *(true: not paraphrase, BERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.729)*

> s1: "Morrill's wife, Ellie, sobbed and hugged Bondeson's
>     sister-in-law during the service."
> s2: "At the service Morrill's widow, Ellie, sobbed and hugged
>     Bondeson's sister-in-law as people consoled her."

A single word change — *wife* → *widow* — implies Morrill is now
deceased, which changes the world-state that the sentence describes.
BERT treats it as a stylistic rewrite; DistilBERT correctly flags it.

### §2.3 Quoted-content edits and speaker swaps

**Case 2.4** — id 251 *(true: not paraphrase, BERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.670)*

> s1: '"We think that the United States of America should support the
>     free speech of all groups," Mr. White said, objecting to Mr.
>     Olson's recommendation.'
> s2: '"We think that the United States of America should support the
>     free speech of all groups," he said.'

The quoted core is identical, but the surrounding context differs
substantively — s1 names two people and a stance ("objecting to..."),
s2 has only an anaphoric "he." BERT scores them as paraphrases;
DistilBERT keeps the surrounding context as part of the comparison.

### §2.4 Different events sharing core vocabulary

**Case 2.5** — id 65 *(true: not paraphrase, BERT: **paraphrase**, conf 0.581)*

> s1: "The Dow Jones industrial average ended the day down 10.89 at
>     9,837.94, after advancing 111.04 Wednesday."
> s2: "The Dow Jones industrial average fell 10.89 points, or 0.11
>     percent, to 9,837.94."

Both describe the Dow falling 10.89 to 9,837.94. They appear
paraphrastic on the matching numerics, but s1 adds Wednesday's prior
advance (111.04) and s2 adds a percentage figure. The annotator label is
*not paraphrase*. DistilBERT's stricter discrimination wins.

## §3. Discussion

The three datasets paint contrasting pictures of the cost of distillation:

| Dataset | Headline gap | Where it hurts most |
|---|---:|---|
| SST-2 (single sentence, sentiment) | BERT +2.87 pp | negation, contrastive structure, sarcasm |
| IMDb (long document, sentiment) | BERT +0.84 pp | buried verdicts in long reviews |
| **MRPC (sentence pair, paraphrase)** | **DistilBERT +1.47 pp** | n/a — the student wins |

What's different about MRPC?

1. **Training-set size.** MRPC has 3,668 training pairs vs. 25K for IMDb
   and 67K for SST-2. BERT has 1.6× as many parameters as DistilBERT
   (110 M vs. 67 M); on a small task that is more capacity to overfit
   with, not more capacity to generalize with.

2. **The dominant failure mode is asymmetric.** BERT is *over-confident
   that two sentences sharing surface material are paraphrases* (71% of
   `distilbert_only_correct` cases are non-paraphrases that BERT
   misclassifies as paraphrases). DistilBERT, with less capacity, is
   correspondingly more conservative — it requires more than a shared
   lexical core to commit to "paraphrase."

3. **Lower agreement rate.** The two models agree on only 86.3% of MRPC
   pairs, vs. 95.3% on SST-2 and 95.2% on IMDb. They are genuinely
   making different decisions on the harder pairs, not the same decision
   with different confidence.

4. **Both models behave similarly on negation and on length.** The
   negation gap (1.97 pp) is close to the no-negation gap (1.17 pp), and
   accuracy is roughly flat across length buckets (0.85 / 0.81 / 0.87).
   So the MRPC effect is not driven by the kinds of compositional
   structure that hurt DistilBERT on SST-2.

So the cost of distillation depends on the task. On a small
sentence-pair dataset like MRPC, the larger model has more capacity to
overfit and the student turns out to be the safer choice. On SST-2 and
IMDb the picture is the opposite: BERT extracts compositional signal
that DistilBERT misses, and the gap shows up in the kinds of sentences
(negation, sarcasm, buried verdicts) where surface-level cues do not
match the label.
