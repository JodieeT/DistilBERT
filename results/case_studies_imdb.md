# Case Studies: Where BERT and DistilBERT Disagree on IMDb

This document is the qualitative half of the IMDb error analysis. Aggregate
numbers live in [`error_breakdown_imdb.json`](error_breakdown_imdb.json) and
the figures under [`figures_imdb/`](figures_imdb/). Here we look at *which
kinds of reviews* drive the 0.84-percentage-point accuracy gap, by reading
individual disagreements between the teacher (BERT-base) and the student
(DistilBERT-base).

The full ranked list of disagreements is generated by
[`code/extract_cases.py`](../code/extract_cases.py) and dumped to
[`case_studies_candidates_imdb.csv`](case_studies_candidates_imdb.csv); below
we hand-pick the most representative reviews and label the linguistic
phenomenon at play. Reviews are truncated for readability — the original
texts are in the CSV.

---

## Summary of phenomena

Out of 25,000 IMDb test reviews, the two models disagreed on **1,190**
(700 BERT-only-correct, 490 DistilBERT-only-correct, ≈ 4.8% disagreement).
The dataset's average review length is 222 words — long enough that *where*
in the review the verdict appears matters. Reading the disagreements, three
recurring patterns explain most of the gap:

| Phenomenon | Cases below | Why DistilBERT loses |
|---|---|---|
| Buried verdict in long review | §1 (3 cases) | the conclusion sits in the last paragraph after extensive plot summary or descriptive praise; DistilBERT averages over the document and is dragged toward the surface tone |
| Temporal / sentiment reversal | §2 (2 cases) | the review opens with sincere praise then pivots ("I loved it as a kid… now I see it's terrible"); DistilBERT anchors on the opening |
| Ironic / understated polarity | §3 (2 cases) | reviewers use ostensibly positive phrasing ("great movie to watch drunk") to convey a negative verdict; surface tokens point the wrong way |

Then §4 covers the reverse direction (DistilBERT correct, BERT wrong),
which is where contrastive structure ("X is a mess, **but** it's an
entertaining mess") sometimes flips the teacher.

One pattern worth flagging across §1–§3: when DistilBERT gets a review
wrong, it is not hedging. Its mean confidence on the 700 `bert_only_correct`
reviews is **0.867**, despite 0% accuracy on those samples. The student
fails silently rather than expressing uncertainty. The same calibration
failure is visible in
[`fig7_confidence_calibration.png`](figures_imdb/fig7_confidence_calibration.png).
And from [`fig3_length_buckets.png`](figures_imdb/fig3_length_buckets.png),
the gap grows from +0.26 pp on reviews under 100 words to **+1.94 pp on
reviews over 400 words** — long-context reasoning is exactly where the
student pays the largest distillation tax.

## §1. Buried verdict in long review

The reviewer spends most of the document on plot summary or descriptive
praise of individual elements (acting, music, costumes), then delivers a
short, decisive negative verdict at the end. BERT integrates the whole
review; DistilBERT reads the bulk and stops.

**Case 1.1** — id 7123 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.998 — 421 words)*

> "This movie has lots of action and little heart. […] He continues to try
> to take advantage of what is obviously a very simple girl. […] **This
> film manages to completely waste the considerable acting talents of early
> talkie actress Nancy Carroll.** She does a good job with what little she
> is given to do, but that is not much. Lilyan Tashman is the standout
> here […] her earthy voice and glamorous looks make her the center of
> attention in every scene in which she appears."

The opening line is the verdict ("little heart"); the body is plot summary
with positive descriptors of two supporting actresses. BERT correctly
weights the opener and the "completely waste" sentence; DistilBERT — at
99.8% confidence — picks up the praise of Tashman and Carroll and calls
it positive.

**Case 1.2** — id 12006 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.996 — 518 words)*

> "[*Aeon Flux*] **isn't nearly the affront to taste and decency one might
> expect** […] it at least has (sort of) an idea with which to toy around.
> Too bad director Karyn Kusama seems to have little clue how to execute
> it all. […] **The big problems of 'Aeon Flux' are technical.** […] Like
> a novice poker player dealt a royal flush, it somehow finds a way to
> lose in spite of its potential."

A backhanded opening ("isn't nearly the affront one might expect" — i.e.
faint praise) followed by mild concession ("it at least has an idea"),
then 300 words on technical failures, ending with a "wasted potential"
metaphor. Even BERT is only 67% confident, but it's correct; DistilBERT
locks onto the early hedged praise and the mid-review "ideas are there,
at least" and goes 99.6% positive.

**Case 1.3** — id 8759 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.997 — 389 words)*

> "**I loved this movie when it first came out** […] as I saw this film
> again many years later, **it hit me… Why in the world did I like this
> film as a Kid, watching it again as an adult I realized that this film
> is terrible.** […] its all ruined by Merle Haggard Narrating everything
> […] this film [is] a beautiful woman dress[ed] in the ugliest clothing.
> I give it 4 stars out of 10."

The first sentence — eight words long — is a sentiment trap. The actual
verdict ("this film is terrible", "4 stars out of 10") arrives a hundred
words later and is repeated, but DistilBERT never recovers from the
opener.

## §2. Temporal / sentiment reversal

The mirror image of §1: a tight, short reversal that BERT reads
compositionally but DistilBERT does not. The structure is "I [used to / on
the surface] X, but [actually / now] Y", with X and Y of opposite polarity.

**Case 2.1** — id 8759 (already shown in §1.3) is the canonical example.
The temporal pivot — *as a kid → as an adult* — flips the polarity of the
"I loved this movie" opener. DistilBERT processes it as two unrelated
positive clauses.

**Case 2.2** — id 24524 *(true: positive, DistilBERT: **negative**, conf 0.996 — 452 words)*

> "Being raised at the time this movie was released has probably influenced
> my shallow mind, but **still, this isn't a bad movie by any means.** […]
> Excuse me for going off on a tangent […] I'm just so fed up with that
> special effects junk. […] **Toy Soldiers is simply a great movie.** […]
> How dare you jaded blowhards rate this movie poorly! Shame on you all!
> Personal rating: 8/10."

Polarity is encoded by the negation ("isn't a bad movie") and a 300-word
defensive rant that contains many surface-negative tokens (*shallow mind,
substance-less, cliched tripe, jaded blowhards, shame*). BERT recognises
that those negative words are aimed at *other* movies and *other reviewers*,
not at *Toy Soldiers*. DistilBERT cannot disentangle the targets and goes
99.6% negative.

## §3. Ironic / understated polarity

Reviewers use ostensibly positive phrasing to deliver negative verdicts —
common in B-movie / cult reviewing, where "great" and "fun" are recycled
ironically.

**Case 3.1** — id 11290 *(true: negative, DistilBERT: **positive**, conf 0.990 — 48 words)*

> "This movie is so over-the-top as to be a borderline comedy. Laws of
> physics are broken. Things explode for no good reason. **Great movie to
> sit down with a six-pack and enjoy. Do not — I repeat DO NOT see this
> movie sober. You will die horrible death!!!**"

A 48-word review — length isn't the issue here. The review uses *great
movie*, *enjoy* and an exclamation-heavy register that DistilBERT reads as
genuine enthusiasm. The IMDb ground-truth label is negative (rating ≤ 4);
the reviewer is recommending it as a "so bad it's good" experience, with
"you will die a horrible death" as the actual verdict.

**Case 3.2** — id 4236 (visible in candidates CSV — 60 words, negative)

> "A clever overall story/location for a story. Action is respectable. The
> children are annoying and their motivation is unclear. […] One of Van
> Damme's better movies."

The review is a list of mixed observations ending with a left-handed
compliment ("one of Van Damme's better movies" — i.e. damning with faint
praise). DistilBERT reads the closing clause as straightforwardly
positive.

## §4. The reverse direction — when DistilBERT outperforms BERT

In 490 cases the student is right and the teacher is wrong. The dominant
pattern here is contrastive ("X is a mess, **but** it's a fun mess"), where
BERT seems to over-weight the negative descriptors that come before the
pivot.

**Case 4.1** — id 16503 *(true: positive, BERT: **negative**, conf 0.998 — 90 words)*

> "**SHOWTIME is a mess, but it's an entertaining mess.** The plot is
> forgettable, the satire is lightweight and the action sequences, though
> well-staged, aren't that exciting. **But I laughed. A lot.** […] Murphy
> delivers the one-liners, De Niro cracks us up […] **If you're a fan of
> De Niro or Murphy, you're going to enjoy this movie.**"

Three negative-sounding clauses (*forgettable, lightweight, aren't that
exciting*) are flipped by three pivots (*but it's an entertaining mess /
But I laughed / you're going to enjoy*). BERT counts negative tokens and
goes 99.8% negative; DistilBERT picks up the pivot.

**Case 4.2** — id 14081 *(true: positive, BERT: **negative**, conf 0.998 — 180 words)*

> "OK, I am a sucker. **I loved it.** I had no expectations and had them all
> fulfilled. **It was a terrible movie. I loved it.** […] Now, the movie is
> terrible. The special effects are terrible. The acting is terrible, **but
> I loved it.** […] You must really love B-Movies to get any enjoyment out
> of this."

Eight occurrences of *terrible* / *bad* / *silly*, but the structure is
"X is terrible, but I loved it" repeated five times. BERT counts the
negatives; DistilBERT picks up the rhetorical structure.

**Case 4.3** — id 17266 *(true: positive, BERT: **negative**, conf 0.998 — 487 words, 13 negations)*

> "**This film is bone chilling in a way that is hard to describe.** […] It
> would be a mistake to call this film prophetic, but it certainly makes
> the common playground wisdom […] come alive. […] **Path To Paradise is a
> must see film** […] this film certainly shows that the various agencies
> that were supposed to protect us did not do their jobs right […] **Path
> To Paradise is well done and gripping.**"

The 13 negations are almost all about the *content* of the film (terrorism,
government failure), not the *quality* of the film. BERT cannot separate
target-of-sentiment from sentiment-bearing tokens at this length;
DistilBERT, perhaps because it pays less attention to negation in general,
ends up making the right call for the wrong reason.

These examples are too few to overturn the headline result (BERT wins
overall on IMDb by 0.84 pp), but they suggest the cost of distillation is
non-monotone — the student does not simply inherit a worse copy of the
teacher's decision boundary.

## Discussion

Across the 1,190 disagreements on IMDb, a consistent picture emerges:

1. **DistilBERT is most likely to fail when the verdict is buried in a
   long, descriptively positive review.** The accuracy gap on reviews
   over 400 words (+1.94 pp) is more than seven times the gap on reviews
   under 100 words (+0.26 pp). When the bulk of the document is plot
   summary or partial praise, DistilBERT's averaged representation drifts
   toward the surface tone and misses the buried conclusion.

2. **The student is overconfident on the cases it gets wrong.** Mean
   DistilBERT confidence on its 700 distillation-cost mistakes is **0.867**.
   This is barely lower than its confidence on the cases it gets right
   (0.990) — confidence calibration is not a useful signal for catching
   these errors, just as on SST-2.

3. **Length is the dominant axis on IMDb, not negation.** Where SST-2
   showed a sharp negation-vs-no-negation gap, IMDb reviews almost all
   contain at least one negation (97%), so the negation flag is much less
   informative — see [`fig4_negation_split.png`](figures_imdb/fig4_negation_split.png).
   The IMDb story is about long-document integration: which paragraph the
   model anchors on, and whether it can update its prior when the verdict
   arrives in the last sentence.

4. **DistilBERT's wins are not random.** The 490 cases where DistilBERT
   beats BERT are concentrated in contrastive ("X is a mess, but…")
   reviews and in long reviews where the *content* of the film carries
   negative semantic load that doesn't reflect the *reviewer's verdict*.
   Whatever DistilBERT loses on long-context integration, it appears to
   compensate slightly on these cases — not enough to close the gap, but
   enough to suggest the failure modes of the two models are partially
   complementary.

The unified picture across SST-2 and IMDb: DistilBERT is fine on
short, sentiment-rich text where bag-of-tokens cues align with the label,
and breaks where sentiment is encoded *compositionally* — through
negation, contrastive structure, sarcasm, idiom (SST-2), or through the
position of the verdict inside a long mixed-sentiment document (IMDb).
